Discover the most remarkable locations

Every place has its own character, atmosphere, and story. From iconic landmarks that have shaped Iran’s identity to quieter places that remain unknown to many visitors, the country is filled with destinations waiting to be explored.

This collection brings together some of the most remarkable places across Iran. Explore ancient sites, historic cities, natural landscapes, cultural landmarks, and hidden gems that each offer a different experience.

Use the destinations below as a starting point for discovering Iran beyond a single itinerary. Find places that inspire you, learn what makes them special, and see where your journey could take you. You can also learn if the locations are listed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites, and use Google Earth links to explore these destinations virtually.

01

Persepolis

Location: Marvdasht, Fars Province, Iran.

Persepolis was the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire (c.550 - 330 BC). It is situated in the plains of Marvdasht, encircled by the southern Zagros Mountains. It is one of the key Iranian cultural heritage sites and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Founded by Darius I around 518 BCE, Persepolis became the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire. The city was later burned and partly destroyed during Alexander the Great’s conquest in 330 BCE, leaving the impressive ruins we see today.

02

Eram Garden (Bāq e Eram)

Location: Shiraz, Iran.

Eram Garden, or Bāgh-e Eram, is a historic Persian garden whose origins may date back to the Seljuk era. The garden took much of its present form during the 19th-century Qajar period, when its elegant pavilion and carefully arranged gardens were developed. Today, it is part of The Persian Garden, a UNESCO World Heritage Site inscribed in 2011. With its mirrored pool, towering cypress and palm trees, and richly decorated pavilion, Eram feels like a garden designed to bring paradise to life.

03

Naqsh-e Jahan Square

Location: Isfahan, Iran.

Built between 1598 and 1629 after Shah Abbas I moved the Safavid capital to Isfahan, Naqsh-e Jahan Square became the heart of the new city, surrounded by magnificent buildings including the Imam Mosque, Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, Ali Qapu Palace, and the Grand Bazaar. Today, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognized for its exceptional Safavid architecture and its remarkable combination of religious, royal, commercial, and public spaces. Stand in the heart of Naqsh-e Jahan and experience the breathtaking “Image of the World” — where turquoise tiles, grand architecture, and centuries of Persian history come together.

04

Nasir al-Mulk Mosque

Location: Shiraz, Iran.

Built between 1876 and 1888 during the Qajar era, the Nasir al-Mulk Mosque was commissioned by Mirza Hasan Ali Nasir al-Molk and became one of Shiraz’s most distinctive examples of Persian architecture. Its famous stained-glass windows transform the prayer hall when sunlight enters, creating the colorful effect that earned it the nickname “Pink Mosque.” Visit in the early morning, when sunlight turns the mosque into a living mosaic of color, light, and Persian artistry.

05

Si-o-se-pol Bridge

Location: Isfahan, Iran.

Built between 1599 and 1602 during the reign of Safavid ruler Shah Abbas I, Si-o-se-pol was designed under the supervision of Allahverdi Khan to connect parts of Isfahan across the Zayandeh River. Its name means “Bridge of 33 Arches,” reflecting the two levels of graceful arches that have made it one of the city’s most recognizable landmarks. As the arches stretch across the river, Si-o-se-pol turns Persian engineering into a scene of pure beauty, especially when its reflection meets the evening light.

06

Golestan Palace

Location: Tehran, Iran.

Golestan Palace is one of Tehran’s oldest historic complexes, with its origins reaching back to the Safavid era. After Tehran became the capital under the Qajar dynasty, the palace was expanded and transformed into a royal residence and centre of political and artistic life. Its most distinctive buildings and decorations date mainly from the 19th century, when Persian craftsmanship began to blend with European influences. Step into Golestan Palace and discover a place where Persian elegance meets the changing face of modern Iran.

07

Arg-e Bam

Location: Bam, Kerman Province, Iran.

Arg-e Bam is an ancient fortified city whose origins reach back to the Achaemenid period, while much of the citadel developed during the Parthian and later periods. Located along important trade routes, Bam flourished for centuries as a centre of commerce, protected by its massive walls and distinctive mud-brick architecture. Today, Bam and its Cultural Landscape is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, inscribed in 2004. Walk through the monumental mud-brick walls of Arg-e Bam and step into a desert city where centuries of Persian history still rise from the earth.

08

Shushtar Historical Hydraulic System

Location: Shushtar, Khuzestan Province, Iran.

The Shushtar Historical Hydraulic System developed from ancient waterworks that can be traced back to Darius the Great in the 5th century BCE, with its present large-scale form completed during the Sasanian era. Its network of canals, dams, bridges, tunnels, and watermills transformed the Karun River into a remarkable system for supplying water and irrigating the surrounding land. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009. Watch the ancient waterways still shape the landscape today, and discover an extraordinary masterpiece of Persian water engineering.

09

Takht-e Soleyman

Location: Takab, West Azerbaijan Province, Iran.

Takht-e Soleyman is an ancient archaeological complex centered around a natural artesian lake, where a major Zoroastrian sanctuary and Sasanian royal complex once stood. Dating mainly from the Sasanian period (3rd–7th centuries CE), the site was later partly rebuilt in the 13th century under the Ilkhanids. Today, Takht-e Soleyman is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, inscribed in 2003. Surrounded by mountains and built around a mysterious ancient lake, Takht-e Soleyman feels like a place where history, nature, and legend meet.

10

Kish Island

Location: Kish Island, Hormozgan Province, Iran.

Kish Island has a long history in the Persian Gulf, with its earliest known mention dating to the 4th century BCE, when the Greek admiral Nearchus visited the region. Once an important centre of pearling and maritime trade, the island later developed into a major tourist destination, especially during the 20th century. Kish offers a completely different side of Iran with its turquoise waters, coral beaches, and relaxed island atmosphere. It is included in Iran’s Tentative List as part of a proposed nomination concerning historic settlements along the northern Persian Gulf coast.

11

Historic city of Yazd

Location: Yazd, Iran.

Yazd is one of Iran’s oldest cities, with a history stretching back to ancient times and growing in importance during the Sasanian period. Built in the heart of the desert, the city developed remarkable ways of adapting to its harsh environment, including qanats, windcatchers, earthen buildings, and shaded alleyways. The Historic City of Yazd became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017. Wander through its golden alleys and discover a city where ancient ingenuity and desert beauty have survived together for centuries.

12

Persian Qanat System

Location: Razavi Khorasan, South Khorasan, Yazd, Kerman, Markazi and Isfahan Provinces.

The qanat is an ancient Persian water system developed thousands of years ago to bring underground water to settlements and farmland through gently sloping tunnels. Still found across Iran today, qanats made life and agriculture possible in some of the country’s driest regions. The Persian Qanat became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016, represented by eleven historic qanats. Hidden beneath the desert, these remarkable tunnels reveal how human ingenuity turned some of Iran’s harshest landscapes into places where life could flourish.

13

Lut Desert

Location: Kerman Province, Iran.

The Lut Desert (Dasht-e Lut) is one of Iran’s most extraordinary natural landscapes, shaped over thousands of years by powerful winds that carved its dramatic kaluts, while vast dunes stretch across its eastern side. Its extreme environment has recorded some of the highest land-surface temperatures on Earth. The Lut Desert became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016, recognized for its exceptional desert landscapes and ongoing geological processes. Stand among the towering kaluts and endless dunes, and it feels less like visiting a desert than stepping onto another planet.

14

Hegmataneh

Location: Hamadan, Iran.

Hegmataneh, ancient Ecbatana, was one of the most important cities of the ancient Median Empire and later served as a major political centre and summer capital under the Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sasanian periods. Its archaeological remains reveal a remarkably planned ancient city, including mud-brick structures that have survived for centuries. Hegmataneh became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2024. Walk among the remains of Ecbatana and discover a city whose story reaches back nearly three thousand years, where layers of ancient civilizations still lie beneath your feet.

15

Alamut Castle

Location: Qazvin Province, Iran.

Perched high in the Alborz Mountains, Alamut Castle was an ancient fortress that came into the hands of Hasan-i Sabbah in 1090, becoming the political, military, and intellectual centre of the Nizari Ismailis. The fortress remained a major stronghold until it fell to the Mongols in 1256, after which it was largely destroyed and gradually became the dramatic ruins seen today. Alamūt Castle and Related Fortifications became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2026. Clinging to a mountain above the Alamut Valley, the ruins of this legendary fortress make history feel as dramatic as the landscape itself.

16

Ali-Sadr Cave

Location: Hamadan Province, Iran.

Ali-Sadr Cave is one of the world’s largest navigable water caves, known for its underground lakes, tunnels, and striking stalactites and stalagmites. The cave was known in ancient times, but its modern rediscovery came in 1963, when a group of Iranian mountaineers explored it. Today, visitors can travel by boat through its vast network of underground waterways. It is currently on Iran’s Tentative List. Glide through its silent underground lakes and discover a hidden world carved into the mountains of western Iran.

17

Jiroft

Location: Kerman Province, Iran.

Jiroft is home to the remarkable Konar Sandal archaeological site, a Bronze Age settlement dating mainly to the 3rd millennium BCE. Excavations revealed evidence of a sophisticated society with monumental structures, distinctive art, and extensive trade connections, offering a fascinating glimpse into one of Iran’s earliest urban cultures. Step into the world of ancient Jiroft, where the ruins of a long-lost civilization are still revealing their secrets beneath the earth.

18

Vank Cathedral

Location: Isfahan, Iran.

Vank Cathedral was established in 1606 in New Julfa, after Shah Abbas I resettled Armenians in Isfahan during the Safavid era. The present cathedral was built between 1655 and 1664, combining Armenian Christian traditions with elements of Persian architecture. Its richly painted interior, filled with detailed frescoes and gilded decoration, makes it one of Isfahan’s most remarkable religious landmarks. Step inside Vank Cathedral and discover a breathtaking world where Armenian art and Persian architecture meet beneath one extraordinary dome.

19

Chogha Zanbil

Location: Khuzestan Province, Iran.

Built around 1250 BCE by the Elamite king Untash-Napirisha, Chogha Zanbil was founded as a sacred city dedicated to the Elamite gods. Its enormous mud-brick ziggurat was surrounded by three concentric walls and remained unfinished after the city was attacked and later destroyed by the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal around 640 BCE. Chogha Zanbil became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979, making it one of Iran’s earliest sites on the World Heritage List. Standing before its enormous ziggurat, it is hard not to imagine the ancient Elamites gathering here more than 3,000 years ago.

20

Taq-e Bostan

Location: Kermanshah Province, Iran.

Taq-e Bostan is a remarkable collection of Sasanian rock reliefs and stone carvings, created mainly during the 4th century CE in the Zagros Mountains. The reliefs depict Sasanian kings, royal ceremonies, and hunting scenes, offering a rare glimpse into the art and royal life of ancient Persia. Taq-e Bostan is on Iran’s UNESCO Tentative List, but it is not currently a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Carved into the mountainside nearly 1,700 years ago, Taq-e Bostan brings the grandeur of the Sasanian kings vividly to life in stone.

21

The Cultural-Natural Landscape of Ramsar

Location: Mazandaran Province, Iran.

Ramsar lies between the Alborz Mountains and the Caspian Sea, where lush forests, gardens, historic buildings, and coastal landscapes come together. During the first Pahlavi period, the town developed into an important resort, with landmarks such as the Ramsar Hotel, Pahlavi Palace, and its surrounding gardens shaping its distinctive character. The Cultural-Natural Landscape of Ramsar is on UNESCO’s Tentative List, submitted in 2007, but it is not currently a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Where mountains meet the sea, Ramsar offers a rare landscape where nature and history seem to belong to the same story.

22

Azadi Tower

Location: Tehran, Iran.

Built in 1971 to commemorate the 2,500th anniversary of the Persian Empire, Azadi Tower was designed by Iranian architect Hossein Amanat. Originally called Shahyad Tower, it was renamed Azadi (“Freedom”) after the 1979 Revolution. Its distinctive design combines modern architecture with elements inspired by Iran’s historic architectural traditions. Rising above Tehran’s Azadi Square, this striking white monument turns Iranian history and modern architecture into one unforgettable symbol.

23

Maymand

Location: Kerman Province, Iran.

Maymand is an ancient village where generations of people carved their homes directly into the surrounding soft rock. Its traditional way of life is closely connected to seasonal movement between mountain pastures and the village, preserving a remarkable relationship between people, nature, and their environment. The Cultural Landscape of Maymand became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015. Step into its remarkable rock-carved homes and experience a living landscape where people and nature have shared the same rhythm for centuries.

24

Bisotun

Location: Kermanshah Province, Iran.

Bisotun is an ancient archaeological site along the historic route between the Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia. Its most famous monument is the Behistun Inscription, commissioned by Darius the Great in 521 BCE, with a monumental relief and inscriptions in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian describing his rise to power and victories over rebellions. The inscription later became crucial to the 19th-century decipherment of cuneiform. Bisotun became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2006. Carved high into the mountainside, Bisotun turns the story of an ancient king into one of the most extraordinary monuments of Persian history.

25

Hormuz Islands — Red Beach

Location: Hormozgan Province, Iran.

Hormuz Island, located in the Strait of Hormuz, is famous for its extraordinary colorful landscapes formed by its unusual geology. At the Red Beach, iron-rich ochre gives the sand and coastal rocks their striking red color, creating a dramatic contrast with the blue waters of the Persian Gulf. Its colorful mountains, salt caves, and unusual rock formations make every part of the island feel completely different from the next. Walk along the crimson shore of Hormuz and discover a landscape so vivid that it feels painted by nature itself.
